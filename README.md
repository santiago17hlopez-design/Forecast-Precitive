# Hierarchical Forecast Engine

Monthly forecasting for business volume series, built for the case where the
forecast has to survive contact with a real reporting pipeline: messy inputs,
a model ranking somebody will question, intervals somebody will quote, and a
breakdown by manager and agent that has to add back to the total exactly.

Sixteen models, rolling-origin backtesting, statistically tested model
selection, empirically calibrated prediction intervals, hierarchical allocation
that reconciles to the cent, and a nowcast for the month in progress.

All data in this repository is synthetic and generated by
`generate_sample_data.py`. No client data is included, and none is required.

---

## Why this exists

Most forecasting examples stop at "fit a model, plot a line." The problems that
actually make a forecast publishable or not are elsewhere:

- **The input is damaged and nobody noticed.** One annual file arrives nearly
  empty. The backtest then stops measuring the forecast and starts measuring how
  well each model reproduces the hole.
- **The ranking is decided by noise.** Two models differ in the third decimal
  of WAPE and the "winner" is declared.
- **The intervals are theoretical.** The model's own variance formula says one
  thing; the error you actually observed says another.
- **The breakdown doesn't add up.** The report shows one number at company level
  and a different number when filtered by manager.

Each of those has a module here, and the reasoning is written into the code
rather than left implicit.

## The hard parts

### Damaged input detection, and what to do about it

`forecast/quality.py`

A year that arrives near-empty is not a business event, it is a file that never
showed up. Left inside the training window, the model learns a 98% collapse and
a 12,000% recovery that never happened. Everything downstream — ranking, WAPE,
bands — then describes the hole.

Detection uses a rolling 12-month median, plus a separate rule for months at or
below zero, because those land before the rolling window has enough history to
flag them and would otherwise pass silently.

When a year is flagged, **the whole year is discarded, not just the months the
rolling window caught.** Trimming only the flagged months leaves the cut in the
middle of the broken year, and the surviving months — a fraction of a normal
month — stay in the training set. That is garbage in the training data under a
different name.

Nothing is trimmed if what remains is too short to cover a seasonal cycle. In
that case it is better to keep everything with the alert in plain view than to
train on 8 months and present the result as a forecast.

### Rolling-origin backtesting, never random CV

`forecast/backtest.py`

Stand at a month, know only the past, forecast forward, step forward, repeat.
The model is re-estimated from scratch at every origin — carrying parameters
across origins is using information from the future.

Random cross-validation is never used. Shuffling months lets a model learn from
later months to predict earlier ones. Every metric improves and none of them
mean anything.

An origin that produces non-finite predictions is skipped rather than recorded.
Multiplicative Holt-Winters returns `inf` on series that pass near zero, and a
single `inf` silently contaminates that model's MAE, RMSE and WAPE.

### The ranking is decided on RelMAE, and tested

`forecast/metrics.py`

MASE divides by the seasonal naive's error measured *inside* the training
window. On a strongly growing series, training and test windows sit at different
scales and MASE inflates for reasons unrelated to the model. RelMAE compares
both models on the same out-of-sample points, so the scale cancels.

Then the gap is tested. `diebold_mariano()` asks whether the difference in
squared error is distinguishable from zero across origins. If `|DM| < 1.96` the
models are tied and the engine says so, recommending the simpler one. Without
this you rank on a third decimal place and call it a decision.

### Intervals from observed error, robustly

`forecast/intervals.py`

The spread comes from the backtest, not from the model's variance formula,
because that captures the uncertainty actually observed — including the part the
model does not know about itself.

What is *not* used is the raw spread. Relative error divides by the actual, so a
near-zero month sends it to thousands of percent, and with an ordinary standard
deviation those few months set the band for the whole series — producing lower
bounds deep in negative territory. For a volume series that is not uncertainty,
it is the hole in the data.

Two defenses: months whose actual is under 10% of the median are dropped, and
the spread uses the scaled MAD (1.4826 × MAD). Two readings are taken — scaled
MAD and a 5–95 winsorized standard deviation — and the **larger** is kept, to
bound without underestimating. The scaled MAD tolerates extremes but collapses
toward zero when errors are nearly identical; the winsorized version keeps every
point and only takes the edge off the tails. On a healthy series both land within
a hair of the ordinary standard deviation.

The lower bound never goes below zero. Monthly volume is not negative; a negative
lower bound only means the sigma went out of range, but on a report it reads as
if the business could bill in reverse.

Annual totals propagate uncertainty with √k rather than summing the monthly
bounds — monthly errors are partly independent, so the annual figure is
proportionally *more* reliable than any single month, and adding the bounds
produces a range so wide it is useless.

And then it is checked: `interval_coverage()` measures how often the intervals
actually contained the truth. A nominal 80% band covering 55% of months is not
an 80% band.

### Hierarchical allocation that reconciles

`forecast/hierarchy.py`

Each forecast is split among managers and, within each manager, among their
agents, by the share each held in the same month of the previous year.

**This is not a per-person forecast,** and the distinction is the point.
Forecasting an individual agent from their own history is not defensible — the
series is short, noisy, and broken by people joining, leaving and swapping
portfolios. This allocates a forecast that already exists so the report can be
filtered, without inventing precision that is not there.

Three decisions:

1. **Signs are preserved.** An agent who cancelled more than they sold enters
   with a negative share, and another can exceed 100%. That is exactly what
   makes the parts add back to the total, instead of over-allocating among the
   positive ones.
2. **The agent's value is computed against the category total, not their
   manager's.** Arithmetically identical — the manager total cancels — but it
   does not blow up when a manager closes a month at net ≈ zero. The *reported*
   share is still the one within the manager, left empty when that net is zero.
3. **Allocated rows carry no intervals.** The range of an allocated forecast is
   not a prediction interval, so those columns stay empty rather than being
   filled with something that would be read as one.

`check_reconciliation()` verifies the parts sum back to the whole. In the sample
run the gap is 0.0000%. This is the check that makes the breakdown trustworthy:
without it, a report quietly shows different totals depending on which filter is
applied.

Allocated rows are tagged `kind="Allocation"` deliberately, so measures filtering
on `kind="Forecast"` do not pick them up and no existing figure changes.

### Nowcasting the month in progress

`forecast/nowcast.py`

Intake is batched, not even — the first business days carry the renewals. So
"40% through the month, therefore 40% of the month" is wrong, and wrong in a
predictable direction.

The maturation curve is the historical median cumulative share by business day,
and it is **validated backwards** against months that already closed. That
backtest is what turns the nowcast from a hunch into a number with a known error
bar, and the error bar is what sets the range.

## Layout

```
config.py                   every knob, in one dictionary
forecast/
  models.py                 16 models behind one uniform (train, h) signature
  metrics.py                MAE, RMSE, MAPE, WAPE, bias, RelMAE, Diebold-Mariano
  backtest.py               rolling-origin evaluation, window comparison, coverage
  intervals.py              robust sigma, per-horizon sigma, interval construction
  quality.py                damage detection, training trim, window adjustment
  hierarchy.py              manager/agent allocation and its reconciliation check
  nowcast.py                maturation curve for the current month
  pipeline.py               orchestrator: history in, one results file out
generate_sample_data.py     synthetic history, including the damage
run_example.py              end-to-end demonstration
```

### Adding a model

Write a function and register it. Nothing else changes:

```python
@register("My model")
def _mine(train, h):
    return np.asarray(...)  # h values
```

The uniform `(train, h)` signature is why this stays a one-function change.
Models needing configuration read it from `active_config()` rather than taking
arguments, so the signature never grows. A model whose dependency is missing
de-registers itself instead of breaking the import — a missing optional model
should cost you that model, not the whole run.

## Output

One file, `forecast_results.csv`, carrying the historical series, the forecast
with intervals, the allocation, and the chosen model's metrics, with a fixed
column order.

One file rather than three on purpose: three files meant three chances for a
report to load a stale one and disagree with itself.

| `kind` | `level` | What it is |
|---|---|---|
| `History` | `Total` | The actual monthly series used for training |
| `Forecast` | `Total` | Central path with 80% and 95% intervals |
| `Allocation` | `Manager` | Forecast split by manager |
| `Allocation` | `Agent` | Forecast split by agent, summing back to the total |

## Notes and limitations

- Monthly data only. Weekly or daily seasonality is not modeled.
- The allocation assumes the hierarchy is reasonably stable year over year. A
  reorganization between the base month and the forecast month will distribute
  to a structure that no longer exists.
- `SARIMA auto` is a small grid search on AIC, not a full auto-ARIMA. It has to
  re-fit at every backtest origin, and that constraint matters more here than
  exhaustive order selection.
- Control-point validation is off until you supply figures from a published
  report. It is the only check that catches a pipeline producing clean-looking
  numbers that disagree with what the business already published — worth
  configuring.
